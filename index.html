How much of your bills does the Singapore Budget actually cover?

When inflation outruns subsidy growth — the usual case — the share of your monthly expenses that government support covers decays geometrically. This page lets you find your household's number, and watch where it goes over the next ten years.

By Ashlyn, Keng Tuan, Lingli · ·

Problem statement

Singapore's annual Budget delivers a bundle of cost-of-living support — CDC vouchers, the GST Voucher (cash and U-Save), and transport top-ups — each tiered by housing type and per-capita household income. The question this page sets out to answer is straightforward to state and surprisingly subtle to measure:

How effective are Singapore Budget cost-of-living support measures in reducing household expenditure across different income groups?

"Effective" here is operationalised as three concrete, measurable things — each addressed by a later section of this page:

  1. Coverage today. At year 0, what fraction of monthly expenses does total support cover? — i.e. S(0) ÷ E(0). Addressed in section 04.
  2. Persistence over time. Does that coverage hold as inflation outpaces subsidy growth? In what year does effectiveness first fall below a chosen threshold τ? Addressed in section 05.
  3. Equity across groups. Do low-income households receive proportionally more support than high-income ones, and does that gap widen or narrow over ten years? Addressed in section 06.

Scope of the model: geometric growth in both expenses and support, a 10-year horizon, and approximate 2025 Budget figures (teaching values, not official MOF amounts). Behavioural responses, in-kind benefits absorbed at the provider level (e.g., subsidised polyclinic visits, HDB grants), and schemes beyond the three listed above are out of scope. The maths underpinning all three measures is laid out in section 07.

07The maths

The whole page is built from three ideas: geometric sequences, the ratio of two of them, and a logarithm to find where one curve crosses a line. Here they are in order — read these and you'll understand exactly what every number above is doing.

1Compounding — geometric sequences

When a price rises 3% in a year, it rises by 3% of last year's price, not 3% of the original. After two years that 3% has been applied to an already-3%-higher base, and so on. This is compound growth, and it's described by a recurrence — each term defined in terms of the previous:

E(n + 1) = E(n) · (1 + i),   E(0) given

A sequence built by multiplying by a fixed ratio is called a geometric sequence. Unrolling the recurrence — apply it once, then again, then again — gives a closed form:

E(n) = E(0) · (1 + i)n

Support follows the same shape with its own growth rate s:

S(n) = S(0) · (1 + s)n

Try setting inflation to 0% in section 5: the expense line flattens, because (1 + 0)n = 1 for every n. Compounding only matters when the rate is non-zero.

2Why the curves diverge — the ratio of two geometrics

The net burden — what the household carries after subsidies — is the difference of two geometric sequences:

B(n) = E(n) − S(n) = E(0)(1+i)n − S(0)(1+s)n

B(n) itself is not a geometric sequence; it's the difference of two. But to ask "how well does support keep up with prices?" the cleaner quantity is the ratio, which we call effectiveness:

S(n) / E(n) = [S(0)/E(0)] · [(1 + s)/(1 + i)]n

This is a geometric sequence in n, with ratio (1+s)/(1+i). The behaviour falls into three clean regimes:

  • i > s (inflation outruns subsidy growth) → ratio < 1, so effectiveness decays geometrically by the same factor each year. This is what you'll see most of the time.
  • i = s → ratio = 1, effectiveness is constant forever and the two lines stay parallel.
  • i < s → ratio > 1, support gradually catches up.

"Erosion" isn't a metaphor here — it's literally multiplicative decay by a fixed factor each year, which is why the effectiveness line in the chart looks like a smooth downward curve rather than a straight line.

3Year-on-year pressure — the discrete derivative

A continuous function has a derivative; a sequence indexed by integer years has a discrete difference. ΔB(n) = B(n) − B(n − 1) tells you how much worse this year is than last year. Substituting the geometric form and simplifying (subtract B(n) from B(n−1), then factor):

ΔB(n) = E(0)(1 + i)n−1 · i − S(0)(1 + s)n−1 · s

Read it as "this year's added expense pressure minus this year's added relief." Because the expense base E(0) is almost always much larger than the support base S(0), and (1+i)n−1 grows faster than (1+s)n−1 when i > s, the first term dominates and ΔB stays positive and grows over time.

If you set i = s and E(0) > S(0), ΔB still stays positive — even matched growth rates can't close a starting gap. But ΔB stops accelerating, because each year adds the same absolute increment.

4Finding the threshold year — solving with a logarithm

Set yourself a threshold τ for "support that's still meaningful" — the page defaults to 5%. When does effectiveness first drop below it? Start by setting S(n)/E(n) equal to τ/100:

[S(0)/E(0)] · [(1 + s)/(1 + i)]n = τ / 100

Rearrange so the power-of-n term is alone on one side:

[(1 + s)/(1 + i)]n = (τ · E(0)) / (100 · S(0))

To pull n out of the exponent, take the natural logarithm of both sides — this is exactly the situation logarithms were invented for:

n · ln((1 + s)/(1 + i)) = ln((τ · E(0)) / (100 · S(0)))

Solve for n and round up, because years are integers and we want the first year the condition holds:

n* = ⌈ ln(τ · E(0) / (100 · S(0))) / ln((1 + s)/(1 + i)) ⌉

This closed form is exactly where the page draws the vertical hairline in the chart. Two edge cases the formula naturally handles:

  • If i ≤ s, the denominator ln((1+s)/(1+i)) is non-negative, the ratio S(n)/E(n) never decreases, and no threshold year exists. The page reports "stays above the threshold through year 10."
  • If the household already starts below the threshold at year 0 — i.e. τ · E(0) ≥ 100 · S(0) — the formula gives n* = 0.

★The whole model on one line

Compounding gives you two rising curves. Their ratio decays geometrically when one rate beats the other. A single logarithm tells you when that ratio crosses any threshold you care about. Everything else on this page — every number, every chart, every caption — is one of those three quantities evaluated at some n.

What this means

The structural finding is simple, and the page makes it visible: whenever inflation outruns subsidy growth — the typical case in any developed economy — support effectiveness, the ratio S(n) ÷ E(n), erodes geometrically. Not linearly. Not at a rate you can dismiss by quoting last year's headline number. The decay factor is exactly (1 + s) ÷ (1 + i), and it compounds on itself each year.

What this page is not: a forecast. The expense baselines are synthetic teaching values, not households surveyed by SingStat; the support figures are 2025-approximate, not what any specific household actually receives; the model ignores behavioural responses, in-kind subsidies (subsidised polyclinic visits, HDB grants, education vouchers) and the dozens of smaller schemes that round out a real Budget package. Real cost-of-living arithmetic is messier than two compounding curves.

What would actually change the picture? Four levers fall out of the maths directly: raise s so it matches or exceeds i; index each scheme to the CPI so s tracks i automatically; broaden the eligible expense basket so a larger fraction of E(n) is reached by support; or shift from cash transfers to subsidised provision, where the price paid by households moves with the subsidy rather than the market. Different levers carry different fiscal and political costs. The model doesn't pick one — but it makes the tradeoff visible.

Methodology & sources

This page is a teaching artifact, not an official tool. Every number is reproducible from the source code, which is a single self-contained HTML file with no external dependencies.

Where the numbers come from

  • Expense baselines (section 2): synthetic, structured loosely after SingStat's Household Expenditure Survey bands. Each baseline is the monthly amount for a reference two-person household; the page then scales by household size and applies a small housing-type multiplier. Treat as illustrative — your real basket is the one you measure yourself.
  • CDC voucher amounts (section 3): 2025-approximate, tiered by housing type. The model uses S$500 / S$400 / S$300 / S$200 for 1–2-room, 3–4-room, 5-room HDB and private respectively.
  • GST Voucher and U-Save: an approximate annualised bundle, tiered by income band and housing type. Real eligibility and payouts depend on per-capita household income, age, and assessable income — set by the Government in each Budget.
  • Transport top-ups: modelled as a flat S$200 per year per household for illustrative simplicity. The actual schemes are more granular and means-tested.
  • PCHI thresholds: S$1,500 and S$5,000 — the cutoffs used by several MSF and Singapore Budget programmes for the "low" and "high" boundaries. Both have moved over time and may move again.
  • Inflation default (3.0%): roughly aligned with Singapore's headline CPI in recent years. The actual MAS Core Inflation differs and is the typical reference for policy. Move the slider and watch what changes.
  • Subsidy growth default (1.0%): a placeholder. The Singapore Budget does not commit to a fixed annual escalation; each year's enhancements are announced in the Budget speech. The default exists so the page has a starting state.

Assumptions worth flagging

  • Both E(n) and S(n) follow pure geometric growth at fixed rates. Real inflation is volatile; the model trades realism for clarity.
  • The household's basket is held constant in real terms — there is no substitution toward cheaper goods as prices rise.
  • Support is treated as cash-equivalent. In-kind benefits (subsidised healthcare, education, housing) are deliberately out of scope.
  • The 10-year horizon is a teaching choice. Beyond ten years the geometric drift dominates anything else; for the qualitative point that's sufficient.
